01 -- Overview

The scale of exclusion

In 2024, nearly half of all local jurisdictions across these three countries lack a single bank branch -- and the problem is getting worse, not better.

That is 6.8 million Peruvians, 18.8 million Brazilians, and 3.1 million Colombians living without physical access to a bank.

03 -- Findings

What the data reveals

Despite two decades of economic growth, the share of municipalities without a bank branch has barely budged.

Source: SBS (Peru), BCB (Brazil), SFC (Colombia). Authors' calculations.

Peru remains the most excluded: 72.6% of its districts had no bank branch in 2024, virtually unchanged from 2002. Brazil showed moderate improvement before stalling. Colombia started lower but progress has flattened since 2016.

Millions of people live in areas where the nearest bank is in another town entirely.

Source: National census projections matched to banking supervisory data.

The population share in financial deserts has fallen somewhat -- driven by urbanization, not by banks arriving. People move to where banks are, rather than banks moving to where people are.

Banks are closing branches, not opening them. Total offices have declined from their peak in all three countries.

Source: National banking supervisory records, branch-level data.

The rise of digital banking has coincided with branch closures, but mobile coverage and digital literacy remain uneven -- raising the question of whether digital channels truly substitute for physical presence.

A cross-country comparison in 2024 shows the starkly different scales of financial exclusion.

05 -- About

About this project

A financial desert is a district or municipality with zero bank offices. This atlas provides the first systematic subnational mapping of financial exclusion across three Latin American countries, using over two decades of administrative data.

Methodology

  • Financial deserts are defined as administrative units (districts in Peru, municipios in Brazil and Colombia) with zero bank branches in a given year.
  • Panel data spans 2002--2024, constructed from national banking supervisory records at the branch level, aggregated to the subnational unit.
  • Population estimates are matched from national census projections to calculate the share of people living in financial deserts.

Data Sources

  • Peru: Superintendencia de Banca, Seguros y AFP (SBS)
  • Brazil: Banco Central do Brasil -- ESTBAN
  • Colombia: Superintendencia Financiera de Colombia (SFC)

06 -- What's next

Toward a global atlas of financial exclusion

This is the beginning. We are building a comprehensive, open, and continuously updated mapping of where banks are—and where they are not.

Expanding coverage

The current atlas covers three countries. Our next phase will extend to Mexico (CNBV), Chile (CMF), Ecuador (Superintendencia de Bancos), and Bolivia (ASFI)—aiming for 10+ Latin American countries by 2027. Longer term, we plan to apply the same methodology to Sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia, where subnational financial access data exists but has never been harmonized across countries.

Adding layers of access

Bank offices are only one dimension. Future versions of the atlas will incorporate:

  • Layer 2: Correspondent agents and ATMs—transactional access points that serve basic payment functions but cannot originate credit.
  • Layer 3: Digital channels—mobile wallet coverage, 4G connectivity, and fintech lending presence—to measure whether digital finance is reaching the territories that physical banks have abandoned.

From measurement to causal evidence

The atlas provides the descriptive foundation. The next step is causal analysis: what happens to local economies when a municipality gains—or loses—its last bank branch? Using the panel structure of our data and the variation in bank entry and exit, we aim to estimate the causal effects of financial desertification on entrepreneurship, formal employment, and household welfare.

Get involved

We welcome collaborations with researchers, regulators, and organizations working on financial inclusion. If you have subnational financial access data for your country, or if you want to use the atlas in your research, get in touch.
